# viz-os: design notes

For people working on the plugin. The skill files never point here: rules an agent needs live in `skills/deck/`.

## Decisions (and why)
| Decision | Why | Where |
|---|---|---|
| The agent writes only `deck.yaml`; one kit draws everything | Style drift in long outputs comes from hand-written HTML/CSS. If design is not in the model's output, it cannot drift. It also saves tokens: a 21-slide deck is about 4k tokens of YAML, against tens of thousands for hand-written HTML. | `kit/`, `SKILL.md` |
| Rules that long outputs forget are lint checks, not prose | Prose rules decay with output length; a linter does not. The checks: every displayed symbol has a meaning, the ladder order, derived columns have formulas, text budgets, checkpoint spacing, abbreviations. | `scripts/build.py` |
| One expression language for derive, tables, calc, equations, chart functions and `{{text}}` | The formula that computes a number is the formula shown to the learner, so the two cannot disagree; numbers are never typed. | `kit/core.js` |
| Aggregates show their recipe (`mean(x)` → (1/n)Σxᵢ → (8+34+…)/6) | The failure in earlier decks was "x̄ = x̄ = 22.5": a number with no visible logic. | `L.tex` in `core.js` |
| Numbers in worked lines carry their names underneath, once per slide | Anonymous numbers ("0.05152 − 0.009313") were the core complaint about earlier calc pages. Labelling once per slide keeps the clutter down. | `opLabels` in `slides.js` |
| Probe before building: goal, pretest, prerequisites, anchors, length | Prior knowledge decides the entry rung (expertise reversal); a pretest helps memory of the questioned material; anchors make the bridges personal. | `probe.md` |
| A `lead` line on each slide ("But…", "So…") | It makes the causal chain visible and catches "and then" gaps. The lint warns when fewer than half the slides have one. | `story.md` §4 |
| Glass for cards, darker plates for data, one warm accent | Apple keeps Liquid Glass for the control layer and dims clear glass over busy content. Learning content needs legibility first, so data sits on plates, and the orange accent marks only what matters now. | `vizos.css` |
| No SVG refraction filter | `backdrop-filter: url(#svg)` works in Chromium and Firefox but not Safari (WebKit bug 245510), and the user views decks on an iPhone. Blur, rim light, sheen and depth carry the look everywhere. | `vizos.css` |
| Two stages: 1600×900 landscape and 720×1280 portrait | Phones are a main viewing device. One scaled 16:9 stage makes text about 7px on a phone; a portrait stage keeps body text at 16px or more. | `deck.js`, `vizos.css` |
| Content that runs long is zoomed down (never below 70%) and the audit reports below 80% | Robust to slightly-too-long slides without hiding the problem. The fit measures each glass card's content against the card's own padding, because a grid or flex child can stick out of its card without changing the card's `scrollHeight`; the audit flags any text that spills out of its card. | `fitSlide`, `L.audit` in `deck.js` |
| Contrast is measured on pixels, against a screenshot with the text hidden | Tinted glass and labels over chart marks are where legibility fails (Apple's own Liquid Glass backlash was about text over busy backgrounds). Per-line fragments avoid false readings from chips beside the text. | `scripts/check.py` |
| YAML 1.2 booleans (`No` stays text) and one-line YAML errors with a fix hint | Both were hit while writing the example deck: `[Yes, about 2×, No, …]` split on commas, `No` became `false`, and a `?` broke a flow map. | `build.py`, `spec.md` |
| Glass read off the iOS 26 home-screen icons, on a plain black page | The user's reference was their own home screen of tinted Liquid Glass icons. On black there is nothing to refract, so the glass is in the light, as on the icons: a body tinted cool blue (`--glass: 124, 148, 255` over `#0b0d15`) that is brightest at the top, dips, and lifts again where light pools at the bottom; a 1px rim bright at the top left and bottom right; a soft glow inside the edge where the glass is thick. Marks on the glass are frosted like the icons' glyphs (bars lit at the top with a highlight line, glass beads for dots, frosted white for context pieces). The answer card is lit orange at the rim and corner instead of filled, because dark orange glass reads as brown. `theme: {glass: clear}` gives neutral smoke glass. History: moonlit, then an orange-glow wallpaper, then proff mono grey; the user asked for a plain, noiseless black page and no orange backgrounds. | `vizos.css`, `chart.js`, `slides.js` |
| Series colours near the iOS system colours: blue `#4699fe`, mint `#13c097`, magenta `#c85eb4` | Found by a search over OKLCH (blue 235–265°, teal 160–200°, purple 295–335°) for the triad with the best worst-case separation from each other and from the orange accent. On the plate `#13151f`, with the accent, all pairs pass the dataviz validator: CVD ΔE 10.8 (worst, magenta ↔ mint, deutan), normal vision ΔE 19.3 (magenta ↔ orange), contrast ≥ 3:1; the same holds on the clear plate `#1a1a1c`. The lightness-band check flags blue, mint and the accent as a little lighter than its dark band (OKLCH L 0.68–0.72 against ≤ 0.67); kept for legibility on dark glass. Grey and frosted white carry context. | `vizos.css`, chart defaults |
| Compact headlines (46px), wider visuals (text column 26%) | The user asked for smaller headings and bigger plots. | `vizos.css` |
| Visual first: the linter counts the words on each slide | The user's feedback: slides were text-heavy and the story should come through the visuals. proff-viz's own floor says the same ("the figure is the page": short labels on the figure, ≤ 40 words of labels per step). Limits: about 40 words on an idea slide, ≤ 2 short points beside a visual, titles ≤ 8 words, captions ≤ 20; an idea slide without a visual is warned. Labels inside a chart, flow or parts bar have their own limits. | `build.py` |
| The formula is the figure: each term is named under it | Names sit next to the thing they name, and the same colour links a term to its number in the worked line. Saying it aloud and the code moved behind "+ more", one tap away. | `slides.js` (`termLabels`) |
| `parts` visual: a whole split into named pieces, or one bar per row | Variance = counting part + wobble part was told in tables and algebra; the sizes were never seen. One bar shows a split; rows show how the split changes (the wobble's share as the mean grows). | `slides.js` |
| No formula from nowhere: `from:` and `kin:` on every equation | The user's feedback: formulas appeared out of nowhere. They wanted an idea of how each one is reached (not the full derivation) and how it relates to formulas in other topics. Each equation shows a short chain from a form the learner knows, one move per link (CPM = a percentage counted per million; the Poisson = the binomial with many rare tries), and 1–3 relatives with the same shape elsewhere (run rate, parts per million). On a wide screen the chain is a column beside the formula that flows down and points across into it; on a phone it is a band above. The linter warns when an equation has no `from`. | `slides.js` (`T.equation`), `build.py`, `story.md` §7 |
| Tables one step at a time | The user wanted step-by-step calculation in tables. Step 1 names the given columns and what each holds; then a column (or a row, or a single number) per step. The cells a formula reads are underlined in the colour their symbol wears in the formula, so each number can be followed from the table into the working (signalling plus spatial contiguity). All steps' working shares one grid cell, so the card never changes size between steps. Any computed number can be tapped for its own working. | `slides.js` (`T.table`) |
| Term labels: above the formula for numerators and powers, below for the rest; slide before dropping a row | Labels under a stacked fraction hung on one vertical leader and the lower leader cut through the upper label. Now the side is set by the term's height against the `=` sign, a label slides sideways while its leader still lands inside it, neighbours are pushed left as a group to open a lane, and a label drops a row only when that fails. Wrapped labels shrink to their longest line. The audit flags any label that still has to cross. | `termLabels` in `slides.js` |

## Evidence behind the story rules
Sources were checked by web search this session; effect sizes are as reported by those sources.
- **Pretesting / prequestions.** St. Hilaire, Chan & Ahn (2024), *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review*, preregistered meta-analysis: specific effect g = 0.54 (k = 97), general effect g ≈ 0.04 (k = 91). Guessing helps memory of the questioned material, not the rest. Narrative review: Pan & Carpenter (2023), *Educational Psychology Review* 35:97. Hence the pretest is about the core mechanism, and the deck reveals it.
- **Comparison and analogy.** Alfieri, Nokes-Malach & Schunn (2013), *Educational Psychologist* 48(2): case comparison beats single-case study (reported d ≈ 0.50; one summary says 0.60). Bridging analogies and "anchoring intuitions": Clement (1993), *Journal of Research in Science Teaching* 30(10). Hence `bridge` slides with explicit pairs and `breaks`.
- **Concrete to abstract.** Fyfe, McNeil, Son & Goldstone (2014), *Educational Psychology Review* 26: start concrete, fade to abstract. A 2020 review questions how far this carries into advanced science (*Educational Psychology Review*, 10.1007/s10648-020-09581-7). Hence numbers before symbols, without dropping the general form.
- **Prior knowledge.** Expertise reversal effect: Kalyuga, Ayres, Chandler & Sweller (2003), *Educational Psychologist* 38. Hence the probe sets the entry rung and skips known rungs.
- **Names first.** Mayer's pre-training principle: learning improves when the names and characteristics of key concepts come first. Hence the `name` move and `symbols` with meanings.
- **Signalling.** Schneider, Beege, Nebel & Rey (2018), *Educational Research Review* 23 (103 studies): signalling helps retention and transfer, more for low prior knowledge. Hence colour-linked terms, the orange accent and the "Look:" captions.
- **Narrative.** Dahlstrom (2014), *PNAS* 111 (Suppl. 4): narrative raises comprehension and engagement for non-experts. Hence the spine and the but/so chain.
- **Comparing cases, and explaining why.** Comparing two analogous cases helps learners pull out the shared structure and transfer it: Gentner, Loewenstein & Thompson (2003), *Journal of Educational Psychology* 95, 393–408 (comparing two cases beat studying them one at a time). Explaining why a fact holds (elaborative interrogation) and self-explanation were rated moderate utility in Dunlosky et al. (2013), *Psychological Science in the Public Interest* 14(1), doi 10.1177/1529100612453266, with larger benefits when the learner has more prior knowledge. Hence `from:` (why this form, from one the learner knows) and `kin:` (the same shape in other topics). Checked by web search on 2026-10-24.
- **Fewer words, labels on the picture.** Coherence principle (leave out extraneous words and pictures): supported in 23 of 23 experimental tests, median effect size 0.86, per Mayer & Fiorella's chapter in the *Cambridge Handbook of Multimedia Learning* (2nd ed., 2014; an earlier count was 10 of 11 tests, median 1.32). Spatial contiguity (put words next to the part of the picture they describe): Ginns (2006), *Learning and Instruction* 16, 511–525, a meta-analysis of spatial and temporal contiguity. Hence the word limits, captions that say where to look, and term names under the formula. These are lab effects on short lessons; none tested viz-os.
Uncertainty: these are lab and classroom effects on average; none tested viz-os itself. The honest test is whether decks built with the probe and bridges beat decks without them for this learner, with the same topics.

## Token budget (measured)
`SKILL.md` ≈ 1.4k tokens (every time) · `probe.md` ≈ 1.1k (first turn) · `story.md` + `spec.md` ≈ 5k (build turn) · the example deck ≈ 4.1k tokens of YAML for 21 slides. The kit (≈ 190 KB) is never read by the agent. Build and check print one line per problem.
